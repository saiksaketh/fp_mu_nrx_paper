\section{Background}
\label{sec:qat_pruning}

This section reviews the MU-\gls{MIMO} uplink model, the convolutional
graph neural receiver that we compress, and the associated training
objective. It then recalls per-channel fake quantization and the
integer and floating-point microformats used as weight grids.
Activation quantization and magnitude pruning, which form the proposed
compression stack, are specified in Section~\ref{sec:nrx_qat}.

\vspace{-2mm}

\subsection{Uplink Setup}
\label{sec:system_model}
$U$ single-layer \glspl{UE} share the same time--frequency resources
toward a base station with $B$ antennas. After OFDM demodulation, each
resource element at subcarrier $s$ and OFDM symbol $t$ observes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mu_mimo}
  \mathbf{y}_{s,t}
  = \sum_{u=1}^{U}
    \mathbf{H}_{s,t,u}\,x_{s,t,u}
    + \mathbf{n}_{s,t},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{n}_{s,t}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},N_0\mathbf{I}_B),
\end{equation}
where $u$ indexes the \glspl{UE},
$x_{s,t,u}\in\mathbb{C}$ is the transmitted symbol,
$\mathbf{H}_{s,t,u}\in\mathbb{C}^{B}$ is its channel vector, and
$\mathbf{y}_{s,t},\mathbf{n}_{s,t}\in\mathbb{C}^{B}$ are the received
and noise vectors. Here, $\mathcal{CN}$ denotes a circularly symmetric
complex Gaussian distribution, $N_0$ is the noise variance per receive
component, and $\mathbf{I}_B$ is the $B$-dimensional identity matrix.
Payload bits are
\gls{LDPC}-encoded and mapped to \gls{QAM} symbols; known
\gls{DMRS} tones enable an initial least-squares channel estimate.
The neural receiver consumes the full post-\gls{FFT} slot (data and
pilots) and emits per-bit \glspl{LLR} for a conventional 5G~NR
decoder.

\vspace{-2mm}

\subsection{Neural Receiver Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
We build on the convolutional graph neural network (CGNN) of
\cite{scamnrx}, designed for flexible MU-\gls{MIMO} \gls{PUSCH}
reception; related deployment studies emphasize tight memory and
compute budgets \cite{wiesmayr2024design}. A small input CNN embeds
each resource element into a state of dimension $d_s{=}56$;
$N_{\mathrm{it}}{=}8$ unrolled blocks then mix information across
users (dense aggregation) and across the time--frequency grid
(separable convolutions); a final dense readout produces \glspl{LLR}.
Separable layers factor into a depthwise spatial filter and a
pointwise mixer; only the latter, together with the aggregation and
dense kernels, is eligible for pruning (Section~\ref{sec:pruning}).
The same weights apply across user counts and bandwidths, so a model
trained on $4$ \glspl{PRB} can be evaluated on a $132$-\gls{PRB} grid.
In the large configuration used here, both the input and state CNNs
have widths $[128,128]$, aggregation uses width $64$, and the readout
uses width $128$.

\subsection{Loss and Training Schedule}
\label{sec:loss}
Training is driven by bit-metric decoding quality with an auxiliary
channel estimate. Let $b\in\{0,1\}$ denote a coded data bit,
$\hat{L}$ its predicted \gls{LLR}, and $\mathbf{h}$ and
$\hat{\mathbf{h}}$ the true and auxiliary channel vectors,
respectively. We minimize
\begin{align}
\label{eq:bce}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}
  &= -\mathbb{E}\!\left[
       b\log\sigma(\hat{L})
       +(1-b)\log\!\bigl(1-\sigma(\hat{L})\bigr)
     \right], \\
\label{eq:mse}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}
  &= \mathbb{E}\!\bigl[
       \|\hat{\mathbf{h}}-\mathbf{h}\|_{2}^{2}
     \bigr], \\
\label{eq:total_loss}
  \mathcal{L}
  &= \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}
     + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}},
\end{align}
where $\mathbb{E}$ averages over data bits and channel realizations,
$\sigma$ is the sigmoid, and $\lambda>0$ weights the auxiliary loss.
Both loss terms are summed over all
$N_{\mathrm{it}}$ unrolled receiver iterations, and $\lambda$ decays
from $0.02$ to $0.01$ across two schedule phases. Each optimizer step
draws a mini-batch of $N_B{=}128$ independently simulated OFDM
slots in Sionna \cite{sionna}, with random active-user counts,
velocities in $[0,56]$\,m/s, and rate-adjusted bit-energy-to-noise
ratio $E_b/N_0$ in a
phase-dependent window of approximately $0$--$15$\,dB. The
\texttt{FP32} baseline is trained with Adam; quantized models
warm-start from that checkpoint.

\subsection{Weight Fake Quantization}
\label{sec:fake_quant}
Let $\mathbf{W}$ denote a kernel (convolutional or dense) and let
$\mathcal{G}\subset\mathbb{R}$ be a finite quantization grid with
maximum magnitude $g_{\max}=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|g|$. We use
\emph{per-channel abs-max} scaling along the output-channel axis:
indices $c$ and $i$ denote an output channel and a coefficient within
that channel, respectively.
\begin{align}
  s_c &= \frac{\max_{i}\,|W_{i,c}|}{g_{\max}},
  \label{eq:absmax_scale}\\
  \widehat{W}_{i,c}
  &=s_c\cdot\mathrm{round}_{\mathcal{G}}\!\bigl(W_{i,c}/s_c\bigr).
  \label{eq:fake_quant}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{round}_{\mathcal{G}}(\cdot)$ maps to the nearest
element of $\mathcal{G}$.

We follow the weight-\gls{QAT} procedure of \cite{saketh_qat}:
fake-quantized weights are used in the forward pass, and an \gls{STE}
updates a full-precision master copy. We replace its learned clipping
bounds with the live per-channel scale \eqref{eq:absmax_scale}. There,
jointly trained clipping bounds could contract the represented range,
and 8-bit \gls{QAT} retained a gap relative to abs-max \gls{PTQ}.
Fixing the scale to the channel extrema removes that degree of freedom
while covering the full dynamic range. Unlike abs-max \gls{PTQ}
\cite{saketh_asilomar25}, which quantizes a frozen \texttt{FP32}
checkpoint once, \eqref{eq:absmax_scale} is recomputed from the current
master weights at every training step; the weights can therefore adapt
to rounding and, for floating-point grids, nonuniform reconstruction
levels. Biases remain in \texttt{FP32}. For INT$b$,
$b\in\{4,8\}$, we use the symmetric narrow-range grid with
$g_{\max}=2^{b-1}-1$.

\subsection{Floating-Point Microformats: FP8 and FP4}
\label{sec:fp_micro}
Unlike uniform integer grids, low-bit floating-point formats allocate
resolution nonuniformly, with denser levels near zero and wider dynamic
range \cite{micikevicius2022fp8,rouhani2023microscaling}. We use the
OCP E4M3 and E2M1 element formats \cite{ocp2023mx}. FP8~(E4M3) assigns
one sign, four exponent, and three mantissa bits, with maximum finite
magnitude $g_{\max}=448$. FP4~(E2M1) assigns one sign, two exponent,
and one mantissa bit, yielding the fifteen-level grid
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp4_grid}
  \mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{E2M1}}
  = \{0,\pm0.5,\pm1,\pm1.5,\pm2,\pm3,\pm4,\pm6\},
\end{equation}
where $g_{\max}=6$. E2M1 and INT4 both provide nominal $8\times$
weight compression relative to \texttt{FP32}, but differ in grid
spacing. All formats use the same abs-max scaling
\eqref{eq:absmax_scale} and \gls{STE} during \gls{QAT}.
